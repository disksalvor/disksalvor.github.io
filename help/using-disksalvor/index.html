Using DiskSalvor

Install and start

DiskSalvor runs on Windows 10 and 11. It needs Python, a free program it's written in.

  1. Install Python 3.9 or newer from python.org. In the installer, tick "Add python.exe to PATH".
  2. Unzip DiskSalvor into a folder on your computer, for example Downloads. Not on the drive you want to rescue.
  3. Double-click Run DiskSalvor.bat. A small black window opens (keep it open: closing it closes DiskSalvor) and your browser shows DiskSalvor. If you close the browser tab, press O in the black window to open it again.
  4. Plug in the drive and pick it under Choose the drive. Windows asks for your OK the first time: reading a whole drive needs it. Disk images don't.
DiskSalvor runs only on your computer. The browser is just its window: nothing is sent anywhere, and no other website can use it.

Want to try it first? Unzip demo\DiskSalvor-demo-failing-drive.zip and open the image with Open a disk image. It's a made-up failing drive, so you can watch a whole rescue without risking anything.

For a Start-menu shortcut with the logo, run tools\make-shortcut.bat once.

Rescue it for me

The simplest way: answer four questions, and DiskSalvor runs every step by itself, easiest files first, copying after each step.

  1. Where should the files go? A folder on a different drive with room for them. DiskSalvor refuses a folder on the drive you're rescuing, and checks the room before each copy.
  2. What matters most? (optional) Click the kinds of files in the order you care about them: Documents, Photos... They're copied first in every round, so if the drive gives out, they're already safe.
  3. How far should it go? Standard doesn't read the whole drive and usually gets almost everything. Thorough also searches the whole drive for files whose index entry is gone: hours on a big drive, and harder on it. When there's no file list at all (the drive was erased, or a format was stopped partway), searching by content is the only way, so Standard does it too (asking first on a drive with signs of trouble).
  4. If the drive turns out to be healthy: stop and say so, or copy the files anyway.

While it runs

Each step shows what it did, with Details read by read. Pause lets the read under way finish, then waits. Stop stops cleanly; nothing is lost and you can continue later. When the drive keeps hanging, DiskSalvor rests it with a countdown and tries again. It stops by itself if the drive disconnects or gets worse.

When it's done

You'll find your files in the folder you chose, with their folders and dates, and in its DiskSalvor folder, rescue report.txt: what was recovered, what's missing and why, and what to do with the drive now.

A round marked Optional isn't needed by anything DiskSalvor has seen, but is there if a file you expect is missing. On a drive that is new or nearly empty, with nothing deleted, DiskSalvor says so first (This drive looks new): there's nothing to rescue unless you expected files on it, and if you did, the searches for deleted and formatted-over files are one click away.

You can stop between steps and come back days later: what was read is kept, and isn't read again. See Stopping and resuming.

What the drive's condition means

After the first look, and again as it learns more, DiskSalvor gives the drive a verdict, a severity and what to do. When the verdict changes, it says so.

VerdictWhat it meansWhat to do
HealthyReads cleanly everywhere DiskSalvor looked, and the health log is clean.Copy what you need. Nothing special.
File system damageEvery sector reads, but the map of the files is damaged (deleted, formatted, or unplugged while writing). The drive itself is fine.Copy your files off first; repair or reformat only after that.
Connection problemThe surface reads fine, but data gets garbled or the drive drops off: cable, port, adapter or power.Another cable and port; a powered hub for a 2.5" drive. Then carry on.
Soft failureMost of the drive reads; some areas are weak or unreadable. Weak spots often read on a later, slower try.Easy files first, then the rounds for the weak areas. Replace the drive afterwards.
Widespread weak spotsTrouble in many places across the disk, not one or two patches.Most important files first. Avoid long whole-drive reads.
DegradingNew bad spots keep appearing: often a weakening read head. Every hour of use can cost data.Only your most important files, now. A lab if irreplaceable files are still missing.
Physical failureClicking, not spinning, not detected, a wrong size, or it keeps disconnecting.Stop powering it on. Only a recovery lab can help.

Why a verdict, not just numbers: the same 10 bad sectors can be a one-off scratch or the start of a head failure. The verdict comes from where the problems are, whether they're growing, and what the health log says. How the health log works.

What Windows has seen

Windows keeps its own record of trouble with every drive: bad blocks, reads or writes it had to retry, sudden disconnects. The first look reads that record (nothing is read from the drive for it) and keeps only what happened while this drive was connected: Windows gives disk numbers in the order drives are plugged in, so DiskSalvor checks which drive had the number at the time. Problems grouped in one place point to the drive itself; on a memory card or stick, often where it was last written, which the drive's own tests can't see. Problems spread all over can just as well be the cable, the reader or the power.

Your recovered files

Files keep their names, folders and dates. Files on unreadable spots are copied anyway, with the unreadable parts filled with zeros, and marked damaged so you know which ones to check.

What each result means

ResultMeaning
okCopied whole: every piece read without a problem, and its size checked against the drive.

If a file of yours with the same name is already in the folder, DiskSalvor never replaces it: the copy is saved next to it as name (recovered).ext, and the report says so.

Files Windows stored compressed (NTFS compression) are unpacked as they're copied. Files Windows stored encrypted (EFS) are copied as they are on the drive: they open only on the Windows account that encrypted them.

File types: what knowing a type adds

DiskSalvor rescues every file it finds, whatever its type. A file of a type DiskSalvor has never heard of comes back exactly like a photo does. Knowing a type doesn't decide whether a file is rescued; it opens up more ways to find it and handle it:

What DiskSalvor knows about a typeWhat that adds
Its name (the extension, like .jpg)It's sorted into a group, so you can choose, say, Photos first: on a failing drive, what's copied first is most likely to come back. It's counted on the rescue screen.
Its first bytes (its signature)It's recognised even when the name is wrong or missing, and a copy that doesn't look like what its name says is easier to spot as damaged.
Where a file of that type endsIt can be found by its content when the drive has lost its entry in the file index (it comes back without its name). Today: JPEG, PNG, GIF, PDF, and ZIP (which includes Word, Excel and PowerPoint files).

Teaching DiskSalvor a new type

On Rescue it for me, open File types: types that are new to DiskSalvor are listed (already rescued, like everything else) with what their first bytes say, taken from a copy already on your destination (nothing more is read from the drive). Send these types to the DiskSalvor team opens an e-mail in your own mail program with the extensions, how many files have each, the guess, and the first 16 bytes of one file each. Never file names. You see the e-mail before it's sent; for text files those 16 bytes can be readable words, so check them.

Safe copy: an image of the whole drive

A safe copy reads the drive once, easy parts first, into one big file (an image) on a healthy drive. After that, everything else happens on the copy, and the failing drive can be unplugged.

When to make one

When the drive is degrading, or when you expect to need several passes (a search by content, say): every pass on the copy is a pass the failing drive doesn't have to survive. For a drive with a few weak spots, the rounds above are usually enough.

Room it needs

Only your data copies the used parts of the drive. On an NTFS destination that takes about as much room as the data; exFAT and FAT32 can't store the empty parts as nothing, so they need the drive's full size. Everything always needs the full size.

It can stop and resume any time, even after a restart: a map next to the image (.dsmap.json) records what's copied. Areas next to a hang are left as "not tried yet"; press Resume later to try them again. Then open the image in DiskSalvor instead of the drive.

Stopping, resuming, and what DiskSalvor remembers

Everything DiskSalvor learns about a drive is saved as it goes: the file list, which areas are unreadable or weak, what was copied where. When you open the same drive again, it offers to pick up from there, without reading anything from the drive.

  1. Stop any time with Stop, or quit with Q in the black window. Closing the laptop is fine too.
  2. Next time, open the same drive. Choose Use the saved results, then continue the rescue where it stopped. For a card, a stick or a drive without a serial number of its own, DiskSalvor first shows the drive the notes came from and asks whether it's the same one; it also compares the drive's first sectors, and never offers one drive's notes to another.
  3. To start from zero (for a test), use Start over and tick forget what was learned. The old notes are kept as a dated backup.
  4. To delete the notes for good (when you hand a drive back to its owner), use Reports & exports › Delete for good. Your recovered files aren't touched.

The notes are kept with the rescue: in the destination you choose, DiskSalvor makes a folder for the drive, named by what makes it unique (its model, size, serial number and exact size), with your files in it and a DiskSalvor folder holding the notes (drive.disksalvor), the drive's log (drive log.txt, every run) and the rescue report. Nothing is saved about a drive before you choose a destination. On this computer, DiskSalvor's own folder (%LOCALAPPDATA%\DiskSalvor: paste that into File Explorer's address bar, or press Open DiskSalvor's folder in Reports & exports) keeps only the list of its rescues (which drive went where), its settings, and its own logs (a new one each start, kept 7 days). So a rescue is one folder you can move, back up or take to another computer, and a new version of DiskSalvor, unzipped anywhere, finds everything from before.

Is it safe? What DiskSalvor never does

  1. It never writes to the drive it rescues. Every command it sends to a drive passes an allow-list first: reading sectors, and reading the health log. Anything else is refused before it leaves DiskSalvor.
  2. It never copies onto the same drive. A destination on the drive being rescued is refused, and so is one Windows can't confirm is elsewhere.
  3. It never replaces your other files. A file of yours with the same name gets the copy saved next to it, as name (recovered).ext. A safe copy won't replace a file that isn't one of its own copies of this drive. The careful retry (round 4) leaves alone any copy you have opened and saved since.
  4. Nothing leaves your computer, and only the browser tab DiskSalvor opened can use it: other websites, and other programs on your computer, are refused.
  5. It doesn't fight a failing drive. Its own time limit on every read (seconds, not Windows' minutes), weak areas skipped and tried last, and it stops when the drive gets worse.

One detail for experts: to ask a drive how long it retries a bad sector (Drive tests › Identify), DiskSalvor uses the drive's SCT command mailbox. Technically that's a "write log" command, but it goes to the drive's command mailbox, writes no sector of your data and changes no setting.

For staff: rescuing other people's drives

A routine for help desks and anyone who rescues drives for others. It keeps the drive safe before DiskSalvor even starts, and leaves nothing of the owner's behind afterwards.

  1. Before you plug the drive in, stop Windows from opening it. When Windows mounts a damaged NTFS drive it replays the drive's journal, which writes to it, and Explorer, search indexing and antivirus all start reading it. Connect your destination drive first, then in a Command Prompt run as administrator type mountvol /N (new drives don't get mounted). DiskSalvor reads the drive directly and doesn't need it mounted. Afterwards, mountvol /E turns it back on. Never click Format, or Scan and fix.
  2. Connect it straight to the computer: no hub, a good cable, and its own power supply if it has one.
  3. Start DiskSalvor, pick the drive and read the first-look verdict.
  4. Healthy, or a few weak spots: Rescue it for me, into a new, empty folder on a different physical drive.
  5. Degrading, clicking, or getting worse: stop, and decide between a safe copy and a recovery lab.
  6. Check the result: open a sample of the recovered files, above all the ones listed as damaged.
  7. Hand the files over, then delete what DiskSalvor kept about the drive: Reports & exports › Delete for good.
Two programs reading one weak drive slow each other down, so DiskSalvor runs one reading job at a time and says so if you start another.

For recovery engineers: how the saved data is designed

Everything DiskSalvor learns about a drive is in one open, documented file you can read with your own tools: drive.disksalvor, in the DiskSalvor folder of the drive's rescue. It's a standard SQLite database: open it in DB Browser for SQLite, the sqlite3 command, Python's built-in sqlite3, or anything else that reads SQLite. Nothing in it is specific to DiskSalvor's code, and nothing in it can run code.

Built to last

The file is designed to stay readable as DiskSalvor grows (new drive types, new file systems, new facts): every table holds documents, a few fixed columns to find things by plus a JSON column (doc) with everything else. New attributes are only ever added; a version that meets one it doesn't know keeps it; file systems are named by plain names (ntfs, fat, exfat, raw) that are never renamed. The format number (in meta) changes only if an older reader would misread something, and newer DiskSalvors read every earlier format. Each save is one transaction, so the file is always the last whole save.

Values JSON has no type for are tagged: bytes {"$b": base64}, sector sets {"$ss": [[start, end], ...]}, sets {"$set": [...]}, tuples {"$t": [...]}. Ids (id, entry, parent) are stored as JSON, so a text id is in quotes.

Examples

Every file DiskSalvor knows, with its size and whether it was deleted:

SELECT part, json_extract(doc,'$.name') AS name, json_extract(doc,'$.size') AS size, deleted
FROM entries WHERE is_dir = 0 ORDER BY name;

Which file is at a place on the drive (here byte 1,048,576):

SELECT e.part, json_extract(e.doc,'$.name') FROM extents x
JOIN entries e ON e.part = x.part AND e.id = x.entry
WHERE x.start <= 1048576 AND x.end > 1048576;

The unreadable areas: SELECT start, end FROM ranges WHERE kind = 'bad';

To pull files out for your own processing, read each file's pieces from extents (in piece order) out of the drive or a safe copy of it: the start and end are byte positions on the whole drive, so they work the same on an image. The full reference is docs/saved-data.md in DiskSalvor's source.

Questions and problems

It says Python isn't installed.

Install it from python.org (tick "Add python.exe to PATH"), then double-click Run DiskSalvor.bat again.

The browser didn't open, or I closed the tab.

Press O in DiskSalvor's black window: it opens DiskSalvor in your browser again.

It says "Open DiskSalvor from its window".

For your safety, DiskSalvor only works with the browser tab it opened itself, so another program on your computer can't use it. Press O in its black window.

My drive isn't in the list.

Press Refresh. If it still isn't there, Windows doesn't see it either: see What's wrong with your drive? (Not detected).

Windows offers to format or scan the drive while DiskSalvor runs.

Click Cancel. Windows pop-ups: what to click.

The drive disconnected in the middle.

Everything so far is kept, and the file being copied when it dropped is never left with zeros in it: it's copied again next time. Plug the drive straight into the computer (no hub) and press Reconnect, or open it again in DiskSalvor, and continue. DiskSalvor checks that it's the same drive and keeps what it learned (its weak spots aren't read again). If it keeps disconnecting, try another cable; for a 2.5" drive, a powered hub.

Can I look at a file before copying it?

Yes: Preview, next to a file in the list, shows photos, PDFs, text, music and video. A file already copied whole is shown from your rescue folder, so the drive isn't read at all. Otherwise a preview reads the file from the drive; on a drive with weak spots DiskSalvor asks first, because copying the file later reads it again. Web pages and drawings from the drive are shown as text, never run.

It's very slow.

On a failing drive that's normal and deliberate: weak spots get a short, patient try instead of minutes of hammering. Copies of healthy areas run at the drive's normal speed.

Can I use the computer meanwhile?

Yes. Just don't open the failing drive in Explorer or another program: two programs reading at once make a weak drive work harder.

A file came out damaged. Can it be fixed?

Run round 4 (careful retry of damaged files): it reads only the missing pieces again. Pieces that never read are zeros; many photos and documents still open with a small flaw.

How do I report a problem?

Email hello@disksalvor.com or open an issue on GitHub. The drive's log helps most (drive log.txt, in the DiskSalvor folder of its rescue; before a destination was chosen, the newest file in %LOCALAPPDATA%\DiskSalvor\logs). It contains your file names, so look through it before sending.
